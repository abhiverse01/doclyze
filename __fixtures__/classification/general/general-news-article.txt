Portland's Old Town Faces Identity Crisis as Tech Money Reshapes Historic Neighborhood

By Michael Chen | The Oregonian
April 2, 2024

PORTLAND — Standing outside the century-old Rose City Bookshop on NW Second Avenue, owner Helen Park gestures across the street to a gleaming five-story building that didn't exist 18 months ago.

"That's a kombucha taproom with a cold-plunge pool on the roof," she says, her voice a mix of amusement and exasperation. "A cold-plunge pool. In Old Town."

Portland's Old Town Chinatown neighborhood, long known for its historic architecture, social services, and occasionally gritty character, is undergoing a transformation that has residents, business owners, and city planners debating the neighborhood's future. Since 2021, more than $280 million in new development has been approved within a six-block radius, bringing luxury apartments, co-working spaces, and wellness-focused businesses to streets once dominated by vacant storefronts and social service agencies.

The latest flashpoint is a proposal by San Francisco-based developer Meridian Properties to convert the 1904 Merchants Hotel at NW 3rd and Davis into a 45-unit boutique apartment building with ground-floor retail, projected rents starting at $2,800 for a studio. The building currently houses the Portland Chinatown Museum and three small businesses that would need to relocate.

"This is erasure," says Dr. Jennifer Lee, director of the Portland Chinatown Museum. "The Merchants Hotel is one of the last physically intact buildings from Portland's early Chinatown era. It's not just old—it's irreplaceable."

But proponents of development argue that the neighborhood needs investment to reverse decades of decline. Old Town's commercial vacancy rate peaked at 34% in 2020 and has only modestly recovered to 22%. Crime statistics show a 28% decrease in property crime since 2022, which neighborhood business association president Tom Wilson attributes partly to increased foot traffic from new residents.

City Commissioner Carmen Rubio, who represents the district, has proposed a compromise: an overlay zone that would preserve buildings on the National Register of Historic Places while allowing new development on surface parking lots and underutilized parcels. The proposal goes before the City Council for a vote on April 16.

For Helen Park, the question isn't just about buildings. "I've owned this shop for 22 years," she says. "I've seen neighborhoods change before. But this feels different. It feels like the neighborhood is being rewritten for people who don't live here yet."
