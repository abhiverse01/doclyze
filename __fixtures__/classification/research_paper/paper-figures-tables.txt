Urban Microclimate Dynamics: A Multi-Sensor Analysis of Heat Island Effects in Coastal Metropolitan Areas

Abstract
This study presents a comprehensive multi-sensor analysis of urban heat island (UHI) effects across three coastal metropolitan areas: Miami-Dade County, Los Angeles County, and the San Francisco Bay Area. Using a combination of ground-level weather stations, satellite thermal imagery, and drone-based LiDAR surveys collected over a 24-month period (January 2022–December 2023), we characterize the spatial and temporal dynamics of UHI intensity at sub-kilometer resolution. Our dataset comprises over 4.2 million individual temperature readings from 847 sensor locations, paired with land-use classification data and building footprint databases. We find that UHI intensity varies by up to 8.3°C within a single metropolitan area and is most strongly predicted by impervious surface fraction (R² = 0.71), vegetation index (R² = 0.64), and building height-to-width ratio (R² = 0.58).

1. Introduction
The urban heat island effect—the phenomenon whereby urban areas experience significantly higher temperatures than surrounding rural areas—represents one of the most well-documented consequences of urbanization on local climate systems (Oke, 1982; Arnfield, 2003). As global temperatures rise, understanding UHI dynamics at fine spatial scales becomes critical for urban planning, public health interventions, and climate adaptation strategies. While numerous studies have characterized UHI at the city scale (see Figure 1 for a review of existing measurement approaches), relatively few have achieved the sub-kilometer resolution necessary to inform neighborhood-level adaptation decisions.

Ground-level temperature data were collected using HOBO MX2301A sensors deployed at 1.5m height on utility poles. Each sensor recorded temperature and relative humidity at 15-minute intervals. Satellite thermal data were obtained from Landsat 8 and 9 (100m resolution) and ECOSTRESS (70m resolution). Drone-based LiDAR surveys were conducted quarterly to capture building height and canopy cover changes.

3. Methods
We developed a spatial interpolation pipeline combining inverse distance weighting (IDW) for ground sensor data with kriging-based fusion of satellite thermal imagery. Figure 3 illustrates the data fusion workflow. The pipeline produces gridded temperature surfaces at 250m resolution for each 6-hour period.

To identify predictors of UHI intensity, we constructed a random forest regression model with 14 features derived from land-use, building morphology, vegetation, and proximity to water bodies. Feature importance was assessed using permutation importance (Breiman, 2001). Model performance was evaluated using 5-fold spatial cross-validation to account for spatial autocorrelation. 4. Results
Figure 4 presents seasonal UHI intensity maps for all three metropolitan areas. The strongest UHI effects were consistently observed during summer nights (Figure 4b), with peak intensities occurring between 10 PM and 2 AM local time—a pattern consistent across all three study areas and both years of observation.

Figure 5 shows the diurnal temperature cycle for urban core, suburban, and rural reference stations. Urban-rural temperature differentials were minimal during midday (0.3–0.8°C) but reached 6.1–8.3°C during nighttime hours. This finding challenges the common assumption that UHI intensity peaks during afternoon heat events.

We identified significant interaction effects between impervious surface fraction and proximity to water bodies (Figure 6). Coastal areas with high impervious surface coverage exhibited moderated UHI effects compared to inland areas with equivalent impervious surface, suggesting a cooling influence of sea breezes that partially compensates for the heat-retaining properties of concrete and asphalt.

Table 3 compares model performance across the three study areas. The random forest model achieved the highest predictive accuracy in Los Angeles (RMSE = 0.82°C) and lowest in Miami-Dade (RMSE = 1.14°C), likely due to Miami's more homogeneous urban morphology and higher humidity, which introduces greater measurement noise.

5. Discussion
Our results have several implications for urban heat mitigation strategies. The strong predictive power of impervious surface fraction (Table 2) reinforces the importance of green infrastructure interventions. However, the nonlinear relationship between impervious surface and UHI intensity (Figure 7) suggests diminishing returns beyond a threshold of approximately 40% impervious surface coverage.

The effectiveness of cool roofs and reflective pavements, assessed through a subset of 34 sensor locations in areas with recent retrofitting, showed average temperature reductions of 1.2°C (Figure 8). While significant, this effect was substantially smaller than the 3.1°C reduction observed at locations with equivalent increases in tree canopy cover, suggesting that vegetation-based interventions may offer superior cost-effectiveness.

6. Conclusion
This study demonstrates the value of multi-sensor data fusion for characterizing urban microclimate dynamics at policy-relevant spatial scales. Our findings support prioritizing vegetation-based cooling strategies in urban heat mitigation plans, particularly in inland neighborhoods where cooling effects of sea breezes are absent. Future work should extend this analysis to additional climate zones and incorporate building energy consumption data to quantify the co-benefits of heat mitigation for energy efficiency.
