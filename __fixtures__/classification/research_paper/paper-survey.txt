A Systematic Review of Federated Learning in Healthcare: Methods, Applications, and Open Challenges

Abstract
Federated learning (FL) enables collaborative model training across distributed data sources without centralizing sensitive data, making it a promising paradigm for healthcare applications where data privacy regulations (HIPAA, GDPR) restrict data sharing. This survey reviews 247 papers published between 2019 and 2024 on FL in healthcare. We categorize existing work along three dimensions: (1) FL algorithms and their adaptations for medical data heterogeneity; (2) application domains including medical imaging, electronic health records, and genomics; and (3) privacy-enhancing techniques combined with FL such as differential privacy and secure multi-party computation. We identify five key open challenges: data heterogeneity across institutions, communication efficiency for large medical models, fairness across demographic subgroups, robustness to adversarial participants, and regulatory compliance. We conclude with recommendations for the research community and a roadmap for clinical translation.

1. Introduction
The increasing availability of large-scale medical datasets has driven significant advances in clinical AI. However, medical data is inherently distributed across hospitals, health systems, and countries, and is subject to stringent privacy regulations. Federated learning (McMahan et al., 2017) offers an alternative paradigm: rather than pooling data, institutions collaboratively train a shared model by exchanging only model updates (gradients or parameters). This survey provides a comprehensive review of the rapidly growing body of FL research in healthcare.

2. Methodology
We conducted a systematic literature search across PubMed, IEEE Xplore, and ACL Anthology using combinations of the terms "federated learning," "distributed learning," "privacy-preserving," and domain-specific terms ("medical imaging," "EHR," "genomics"). Our search yielded 247 papers after deduplication and inclusion/exclusion screening. We classified each paper along the three dimensions described above.

3. FL Algorithms for Healthcare
3.1 Standard Algorithms. FedAvg remains the most widely used baseline (89% of reviewed papers). FedProx (Li et al., 2020) is the second most common, particularly in EHR applications where data heterogeneity is severe.

3.2 Personalization. A significant trend (34% of 2023-2024 papers) is toward personalized FL, where each institution maintains a locally adapted model. Methods include Meta-Fed (Fallah et al., 2020), Per-FedAvg (Arivazhagan et al., 2019), and Fine-tuning-based personalization.

3.3 Communication Efficiency. Medical models (especially in imaging) are large. Communication-efficient methods including gradient compression (88% sparsity), knowledge distillation, and split learning are increasingly adopted.

4. Application Domains
4.1 Medical Imaging (42% of papers). The most mature application area. Multi-institutional FL has been demonstrated for chest X-ray classification, brain tumor segmentation, mammography screening, and retinal disease detection. Notable multi-site studies include the EXAM consortium (2,700+ patients across 5 continents) and the FeTS initiative (30+ institutions).

4.2 Electronic Health Records (31%). Applications include mortality prediction, disease progression modeling, and adverse event prediction. Key challenges include variable coding systems (ICD-9 vs. ICD-10) and differing feature sets across EHR vendors.

4.3 Genomics (12%). Federated genome-wide association studies (GWAS) and pharmacogenomic model training represent an emerging area with unique challenges around extremely high dimensionality.

5. Privacy Enhancements
Differential privacy is the most common privacy enhancement (48% of papers), with typical epsilon values ranging from 1 to 10. Secure multi-party computation (SMPC) and homomorphic encryption are used in 22% of papers, though at significant computational overhead.

6. Open Challenges
We identify: (a) non-IID data across institutions degrades model quality; (b) large foundation models require novel FL approaches; (c) fairness auditing is underexplored; (d) poisoning attacks by malicious clients remain a threat; (e) regulatory frameworks (FDA software as a medical device) have not yet adapted to federated models.

7. Conclusion
Federated learning has matured from proof-of-concept to multi-site clinical validation in several medical imaging domains. However, significant challenges remain before FL-trained models can achieve regulatory approval and routine clinical deployment.
