Reckoning with Ruin: Collective Memory and the Industrial Landscapes of Post-Socialist Romania

Abstract
This article examines the transformation of Romania's industrial heritage sites into spaces of collective memory and cultural reinvention following the collapse of state socialism in 1989. Drawing on eighteen months of ethnographic fieldwork in the Jiu Valley coal mining region and the city of Reșița, I argue that the physical remnants of socialist industrialization have become contested sites where communities negotiate narratives of loss, pride, and identity. Through analysis of grassroots preservation movements, artistic interventions, and municipal redevelopment projects, I demonstrate how industrial ruins function not merely as obstacles to progress but as active agents in the production of post-socialist subjectivity.

1. Introduction
When the Resita steelworks ceased operations in 2012 after 250 years of continuous production, the towering blast furnaces that had dominated the city's skyline for generations fell silent. For residents of Reșița, the closure was not merely an economic event but an existential rupture. The steelworks had employed over 20,000 workers at its peak, shaping the social fabric, architectural character, and collective identity of the city. Yet within months, municipal authorities proposed demolishing the furnaces and replacing the 40-hectare site with a shopping mall and residential complex.

This article examines the conflicts that emerged from this proposal and similar ones across Romania's deindustrializing landscape. I situate these conflicts within broader theoretical frameworks of collective memory (Halbwachs, 1992), heritage studies (Smith, 2006), and the anthropology of postsocialism (Verdery, 1996; Humphrey, 2005). My central claim is that the industrial ruins of postsocialist Romania constitute what Pierre Nora (1989) termed "lieux de mémoire"—sites of memory that crystallize and express collective identity precisely because the living memory they once anchored is in the process of disappearing.

2. Historical Context
Romania's industrialization campaign under Nicolae Ceaușescu (1965-1989) was among the most ambitious in the Eastern Bloc. The state invested enormous resources in heavy industry, building massive steel mills, chemical plants, and mining operations. These facilities were not only economic engines but also ideological instruments, designed to demonstrate the superiority of socialist production and to create a "new socialist man" (Burdett & Beasley-Murray, 2014).

3. Fieldwork and Methods
I conducted eighteen months of ethnographic fieldwork between March 2021 and August 2022, divided between the Jiu Valley mining communities and Reșița. My methods included participant observation at community meetings and preservation events, semi-structured interviews with 67 residents (former workers, municipal officials, artists, and activists), and archival research in municipal and trade union records.

4. The Steelworks as Memory Site
In Reșița, the preservation movement coalesced around the former steelworks. I trace how a group of retired engineers and former workers formed the association "Reșița Industrial Heritage" and, in collaboration with a Bucharest-based architecture collective, proposed converting the main furnace hall into a museum and cultural center. Their proposal drew on the "ruin value" concept (Benjamin, 1977) to argue that the industrial structures possessed aesthetic and historical significance independent of their original function.

5. Conclusion
The industrial landscapes of postsocialist Romania reveal the complex entanglement of materiality, memory, and power. As these sites are demolished, preserved, or transformed, they continue to shape the narratives through which communities understand their past and imagine their future.
